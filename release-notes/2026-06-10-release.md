# Headline

## To drink, or not to drink?

Your mouth is dry? You may be thirsty. It has long been thought that the sensation of dry mouth and throat was the primary manifestation of thirst, but it is much more complex. Thirst is triggered when there is a rise in plasma osmolality or a reduction in plasma volume or pressure. It is the [brain that responds to deficits in water homeostasis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27991901,25624099,29489747,29268095) and induces drinking behaviour, effectively bringing the blood metrics back to their normal physiological level. More precisely it is the neural circuits in the [lamina terminalis (LT)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamina_terminalis), that underlie thirst perception and drinking behaviour. There are three nuclei in the LT, the subfornical organ (SFO), the organic vasculosum lamina terminalis (OVLT) and the median preoptic nucleus, all of which are anatomically interconnected. Interestingly, SFO and OVLT are located outside the blood-brain barrier and therefore have direct access to the circulation for plasma osmolarity sensing. However, the identity of the molecular 'thirst sensor(s)' remained elusive until recently.

Several months ago, the mystery was finally unveiled in [two publications](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38172113,40107268). The neuronal osmosensor is the mechanosensitive cation channel [TMEM63B](https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/?query=gene:tmem63b+and+reviewed:true). TMEM63B is expressed in the majority of the SFO excitatory neurons. While TMEM63B-deficient neurons are healthy and have a normal response to non-osmolarity stimuli, they do not respond to hyperosmolarity. On the other hand, human TMEM63B can generate hyperosmolarity-induced currents, when transfected into completely unrelated cells, such as Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells. Under normal conditions, TMEM63B knockout mice behave similarly to wild-type littermates, but when tested in either an osmotic thirst model or dehydrated thirst model, they drink significantly less. It should be noted, however, that the water intake in knockout animals under both stress conditions is still larger than that in water-sated control mice. This suggests that TMEM63B is not the only actor in this process and that alternative pathways must also be involved. Hence although a major step has been taken, we may still expect some exciting findings in the field of thirst perception.

These two reports are not the first about TMEM63B acting as an osmosensor. In 2020, TMEM63B was found to be an [osmosensor required for survival of cochlear outer hair cells upon hearing onset](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32375046/). In this context, it was activated by hypotonic stress, and its deficiency caused hearing loss. The involvement of other [TMEM63 family members](https://www.tcdb.org/search/result.php?tc=1.A.17) in the perception of the environment and the body's needs has also been documented in very different experimental settings. For instance, in Drosophila, the TMEM63 channel detects [environmental humidity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35780140/) and is involved in [food texture preference](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33657409/).

While waiting for some new discoveries about TMEM63 family members, why not have a drink and look up the recently updated [UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot TMEM63B entries](https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb/?query=gene:tmem63b+and+reviewed:true?)
